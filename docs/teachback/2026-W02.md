## A → B: element capture and the Spike A corpus

*Notes written by B (Nikhil)*

**What Rohan built (A2.1, A2.2, A2.3):**

`node-worker/crawler/elements.ts` takes a CSS selector and produces a cropped
PNG of that one element, plus its rectangle in two different coordinate
systems at once:

- **CSS pixels** — what `getBoundingClientRect()` and every CSS selector talk
  in. Device-independent.
- **Device pixels** — what the PNG is actually made of. On a 2× Retina
  display a 100×20 CSS-pixel button is 200×40 real pixels in the image.

Both are stored, along with the `device_pixel_ratio` they're related by. The
reason is that the two consumers disagree: the vision model looks at the PNG
and reasons in device pixels, while the contrast maths has to map back to a
CSS selector. If we stored one and multiplied later, every consumer would
have to remember to do it, and the one that forgets produces a box that's
off by a factor of two.

`measure()` adds `window.scrollX/scrollY` back onto the rect, because
`getBoundingClientRect()` is relative to the *viewport* but the full-page
screenshot is relative to the *page*. Without that, anything below the fold
is measured in the wrong place.

`captureElement()` returns `null` instead of throwing when it can't shoot an
element — invisible, zero-size, detached, off-screen, or an axe selector we
can't re-parse. A missing element is an ordinary thing on a real page, not a
reason to fail an entire render.

`imageInventory()` finds every image-bearing element via four routes:
`<img>`, `<canvas>`, inline `<svg>`, and CSS `background-image`. The last one
is the interesting one — text over a CSS background image is exactly where
contrast failures hide, and it's the case axe-core marks `incomplete`.

`render.ts` populates `element_screenshots` for every image at render time;
`static/axe.ts` adds crops for the text nodes axe marks `incomplete`, because
those aren't known until axe has actually run.

The corpus (A2.3) takes 30 clean pages, applies 3 injectors, and produces
labelled cases with known ground truth. Of 197 structurally valid cases,
**117 are confirmed usable** — the rest were injected but the resulting
defect wasn't actually detected, so they can't be scored.

**Thin spots — things I could not explain unaided:**

- How the `.verity/cache/<sha256>/` cache key is derived, and what makes it
  stable across runs.
- Why `captureElements` is sequential rather than concurrent (comment says
  contention and flaky crops — I'd want to see the failure to believe it).
- How the 197 → 117 verification step decides a case is "detected".

## B → A: vision judgments, latency, and the trust machinery

*Notes on Nikhil's Track-B work.*

Track B this week was almost entirely about one idea: **the model is
allowed to be uncertain, and the system is built so that its uncertainty
is the safe outcome.** Everything below is a variation on that.

### 1. Three vision judgments, and why every one can say `unknown`

`verity/agents/vision.py` defines three judgments the vision model makes,
each a Pydantic schema paired with a rubric (the system prompt):

| Judgment | Answers | Escape |
|---|---|---|
| alt-text meaningfulness | `yes` / `no` / `unknown` | `unknown` |
| focus-visible (before/after) | `yes` / `no` / `unknown` | `unknown` |
| contrast-region localisation | `yes` / `unknown` | `unknown` (no boxes) |

The `unknown` value is **load-bearing, not a convenience.** An
accessibility finding that is wrong is worse than one that is missing:
the whole product is gated on *precision* (few false positives), because
a tool that "cries wolf" gets uninstalled. So every judgment must have a
way to decline. A model forced to pick `yes`/`no` on an image it can't
read will pick one anyway — and half the time it's wrong, which is
exactly the false positive we cannot afford. Remove `unknown` and you
convert honest uncertainty into confident error.

### 2. The fabrication trap — and why "well-formatted" is not "true"

To get reliable JSON out of a model you *constrain decoding*: the model
is only allowed to emit tokens that keep the output schema-valid. That
guarantees the shape of the answer. It does **not** guarantee the answer
is true — and worse, it actively hurts.

If a schema marks a field required and the image gives no evidence for
it, the model can't leave it blank (that would break the schema), so it
**invents a value**, fluently and confidently. Constrained decoding turns
"I don't know" into a well-formatted lie, which is more dangerous than a
malformed one because it passes validation and looks authoritative.

The defence is two-part, and both parts matter:
1. **A legal escape must exist in the schema** — every judgment field is
   nullable / carries `unknown`, so the model *can* decline without
   breaking the shape.
2. **The model must actually use it** — and this is where Spike A bit us.
   The rubrics spend most of their words teaching *when* to answer
   `unknown`, precisely to push the model toward it. Our measurement
   found the 7B model went too far the other way: it abstained on 79% of
   cases and never once flagged a placeholder alt. Over-abstention is the
   *safe* failure (it never lied), but it means the escape value is only
   half the battle — a small model can be so cautious it produces nothing
   useful. That is the honest Week-2 finding, recorded in
   [`../measurements/spike-a.md`](../measurements/spike-a.md).

### 3. Contrast-region localisation returns boxes, never a ratio

`ContrastRegionLocalisation` gives back *bounding boxes* — where the text
is, where its background is — and has **no field in which a contrast
ratio, colour, or pass/fail could be reported.** That is deliberate and
it is the single most important design rule in the project:

> **The model localises; the maths decides.**

A vision model is good at "the text is roughly here." It is bad, and
unaccountable, at "the contrast is 3.1:1." So we never let it produce a
number that determines a verdict. The model points; deterministic WCAG
maths (`verity/agents/contrast.py`, and the pixel sampling on the Node
side) computes the actual ratio from real pixels. If the schema had a
`ratio` field, a model could fabricate one and it would flow through as
if it were measured — so the field simply does not exist. A
`model_validator` also ties the two together: `located: "yes"` requires
both boxes, `located: "unknown"` forbids them, so the model can't return
a "found it" with nothing found or vice versa.

### 4. Why finding ids stopped using Python's `hash()`

A finding's signature is `sha256("<sc_id>|<selector>|<rule_id>")`
(`validator/dedup.py`, and the id in `orchestrator/main.py`). It used to
lean on Python's built-in `hash()` — and that's a real bug, subtle
enough to note.

CPython **salts string hashing per process** (PEP 456): `hash("#btn")`
returns a *different number every time the program runs*, unless you pin
`PYTHONHASHSEED`. That randomisation is a security feature, but it means
any id built from `hash()` is not reproducible across runs. Two things
depend on stable signatures: **dedup** (the same defect seen twice must
collapse to one) and **waivers** (a waiver is keyed to a finding's
signature and has to keep matching it tomorrow). Both silently break if
the signature changes between runs. `sha256` is deterministic, so the
same finding always produces the same id — the property those two
features are built on.

### 5. Provenance, and why `cantTell` can't carry confidence 1.0

**Provenance** is the label on every finding saying *how much to trust
it*: `authoritative` (a deterministic engine or pure maths — may fail a
build), `ai_assisted` (a calibrated model judgment — annotates only), or
`needs_review` (insufficient evidence — reported, never gates). It is a
**required field with no default**, because a default is a silent
decision, and provenance is the one thing in this product that has to be
exactly true.

`flag_needs_review()` (in `contrast.py`) is the sharp edge of this. When
axe marks a contrast result `incomplete` — text over an image, where axe
*could not decide* — the finding arrives from the mapper still wearing
authoritative metadata: `provenance=AUTHORITATIVE`, `confidence.score=1.0`,
`method="deterministic"`. Leaving that in place would ship a finding that
says "I can't tell" while also claiming full deterministic confidence —
a contradiction. So `flag_needs_review` rewrites all of it together:
provenance → `NEEDS_REVIEW`, outcome → `cantTell`, and crucially
**confidence.score → 0.0** with `method="axe-incomplete"`. You cannot be
certain and unsure at the same time, and the data model is not allowed to
claim you are.

### 6. Dedup, waivers, and latency (the rest of the trust machinery)

- **Dedup** (`validator/dedup.py`) merges findings by signature so the
  same defect reported by two agents becomes one, strongest-provenance
  wins.
- **Waivers** (`waivers.yaml` + dedup) let a team accept a specific
  finding by its signature. The shipped default waives *nothing* — a
  waiver is a per-project decision and must never suppress out of the
  box. (Expiry is recorded but not yet enforced; that's B7.3, Week 7.)
- **Latency** (B2.5) is split into `render_seconds` /
  `analysis_seconds` / `total_seconds` on the report, not one number,
  because the descope response differs by culprit: a slow *render* is
  Week 17's caching problem, a slow *analysis* is not.
